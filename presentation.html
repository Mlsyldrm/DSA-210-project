01 · About the project

Does ethical consumption deliver a measurable benefit?

As the skincare industry grows rapidly, consumer demand for cruelty-free (not tested on animals) products keeps rising. But does this ethical preference actually reflect on product price, popularity, or customer satisfaction?

Research question

Is the cruelty-free status of a skincare product significantly associated with its price, user rating, and popularity? If so, how can this relationship be characterized in terms of direction and magnitude?

Approach

I first explored the distributions of the variables and their pairwise relationships through exploratory data analysis (EDA). Five hypotheses were then formally tested using non-parametric methods. Finally, I evaluated predictive power with linear regression, Random Forest, KNN, and Logistic Regression models.

04 · Hypothesis tests

Five questions, five statistical tests

Because the variable distributions are non-normal, all tests use non-parametric methods: Spearman for correlations and the Mann-Whitney U test for two-group comparisons. Results are interpreted not only through p-values but also effect sizes.

i.

Price & rating

H₀: No relationship between price and rating. H₁: Higher-priced products receive lower ratings.

H₀ rejected ρ = −0.143 · p = 0.007 Weak but significant negative relationship.
ii.

Price & popularity

H₀: No relationship between price and popularity. H₁: Higher-priced products receive fewer "loves."

H₀ rejected ρ = −0.387 · p < 0.001 The strongest association in the analysis.
iii.

Popularity & rating

H₀: No relationship between popularity and rating. H₁: More popular products receive higher ratings.

H₀ rejected ρ = +0.150 · p = 0.005 Weak but significant positive relationship.
iv.

CF & rating

H₀: CF and non-CF products have the same rating. H₁: The two groups differ significantly.

Failed to reject H₀ U = 8970 · p = 0.357 CF status does not affect rating.
v.

CF & price

H₀: CF and non-CF products are priced equally. H₁: CF products are cheaper.

Failed to reject H₀ U = 10540 · p = 0.086 Insufficient statistical evidence.
05 · Machine learning

Is it predictable? Four models, four experiments

Correlation analysis confirmed that relationships exist. The question now is whether those relationships are strong enough to predict outcomes from product features. Spoiler: features are weak and the signal is limited — but the directions check out.

Model 01 · Linear regression

Predicting rating

Using log_price, log_loves_count, and a skin-compatibility score, I tried to predict product rating. On a single train-test split, R² came out negative — the model performed worse than just predicting the mean.

Performance improved slightly after 5-fold cross-validation, but R² still sits near zero. The reason is clear: ratings are already compressed into a 4.0–4.6 band, leaving very little variance to predict.

06 · Three key findings

The short version of what the data said

i.

Expensive doesn't mean loved

The negative relationship between price and popularity (ρ = −0.39) is the single strongest finding in the analysis. On average, consumers engage less with pricier products — or popular brands keep their prices accessible.

ii.

Ethics ≠ premium

Cruelty-free products are neither significantly more expensive nor significantly cheaper. They don't get higher ratings either. The ethical choice has no measurable effect on pricing or satisfaction.

iii.

Ratings are already high

95% of product ratings fall between 4.0 and 4.6. This narrow variance makes it nearly impossible for any feature to show meaningful "predictive power" over rating — a structural limit of store-based rating systems.

07 · Limitations

Where this analysis stops

No study speaks the final word on its subject, and this one is no exception. The findings should be read together with the constraints that shaped them — limits in data, methodology, and scope that may have influenced what the analysis could and could not see.

— 01 — Small and imbalanced sample
With 293 products and only 99 cruelty-free, both the overall sample and the minority class are small. This limits statistical power, makes effect sizes harder to detect, and constrains classification models trying to learn the minority class.
— 02 — Single-retailer bias
Product data comes from a single retailer (Sephora). The catalogue is filtered by that retailer's curation, pricing strategy, and customer base. Results may not generalize to drugstore brands, indie labels, or international markets.
— 03 — CF labels come from a brand list
Cruelty-free status was assigned at the brand level using a public reference list, not from official certifications (Leaping Bunny, PETA). "Unknown" brands were treated as non-CF, which may overstate the non-CF group and add label noise.
— 04 — Ratings are heavily compressed
Roughly 95% of ratings fall between 4.0 and 4.6. This is a known feature of e-commerce review systems, but it cripples regression: the target barely moves, so even meaningful features cannot explain its variance.
— 05 — Correlation, not causation
Every significant finding here is an association. Lower prices don't cause higher popularity; both may be driven by brand recognition, marketing budget, packaging, or social media presence — none of which are in the dataset.
— 06 — Limited feature set
Only a handful of features were available: price, popularity, rating, skin type, category, CF status. Stronger predictors — ingredient list, brand age, marketing spend, review text, packaging — were not in the dataset and could change the picture significantly.
— 07 — Snapshot in time
The dataset captures a single point in time. Prices change, products go viral, brands gain or lose CF certification. A longitudinal view would tell a richer story about how the ethics–popularity–price relationship evolves.
— 08 — No demographic context
The data contains no information about who buys or rates these products. Consumer demographics — age, income, geography, values — likely interact with cruelty-free preferences in ways this analysis simply cannot observe.
08 · Conclusion

Is ethics still an unmeasured value?

The core takeaway: being cruelty-free does not make a skincare product more expensive, more popular, or better rated. As an ethical choice it retains its value — but that choice leaves no measurable footprint on market metrics.

At the same time, the strong negative relationship between price and popularity stands out as the most informative finding for understanding consumer behavior. In skincare, accessible pricing is more decisive for popularity than almost anything else captured here.

— 01 —

Statistical significance ≠ practical importance

Three hypotheses were rejected; yet all the correlations are weak. Significance speaks to existence, not magnitude.

— 02 —

Narrow variance kills predictive power

With ratings clustered between 4.0 and 4.6, regression models had little to work with. The structure of the data shaped the outcome.

— 03 —

A larger, more balanced dataset is needed

99 cruelty-free products against 194 others constrains the signal that classifiers can pick up. A broader dataset may tell a different story.

— 04 —

Next step: NLP on product content

This analysis works at the brand level; text analysis of product descriptions and ingredient lists could provide a much richer feature set.
